<Hook>Your ranker is perfect, but the item a user wants never reaches it. Where did the system fail?</Hook>

<Intuition>
A recommender is a sequence of narrowing decisions. Imagine a librarian: first find shelves that might contain useful books, then compare a small stack carefully, then remove unavailable books and avoid handing you five near-identical titles. Candidate generation, ranking and policy constraints have different objectives and latency budgets. A later stage cannot rescue a relevant item discarded earlier.

A **two-tower model** separately maps user context and item content into vectors. Their inner product is cheap to compare, and catalog vectors can be precomputed. Our smallest honest version learns ID embeddings on both sides. It cannot infer the taste of a never-seen user from nothing. Popularity is therefore both a baseline and an explicit cold-start fallback, not an embarrassing detail to hide.

A graph adds a second intuition: people who connect to related items may benefit from sharing neighborhood information. But a heldout interaction is an answer key. Including it in the graph—even without its rating—reveals information the model should not have.

<IntuitionCallout>Make four moves: define what was knowable at prediction time; retrieve with a trainable score; preserve the full relevant set in evaluation; compare against an uncomplicated baseline before adding graph complexity.</IntuitionCallout>

## The math, derived

### 1. Two independent encoders make retrieval cheap

Let u be user features or an ID, i be item features or an ID, and d the embedding dimension. Encoders with parameters θ and φ return vectors in the same space:

<MathBlock latex="p_u=f_\theta(u)\in\mathbb R^d,\qquad q_i=g_\phi(i)\in\mathbb R^d,\qquad s(u,i)=p_u^\top q_i=\sum_{j=1}^d p_{uj}q_{ij}" />

For p = [1,2], positive-item q⁺ = [2,1] and negative-item q⁻ = [1,0], scores are 4 and 1. The margin is 3. These unnormalized inner products are neither probabilities nor cosine similarities. Normalizing vectors changes the objective and the ranking; do not do it silently at index-build time.

A production feature tower can encode an item's text/category and a user's recent activity. Independent encoding makes catalog precomputation possible but restricts arbitrary user-item interactions. A later neural reranker or cross-encoder spends more compute on a smaller candidate set. Train/serve feature parity and version-compatible item indexes are part of model correctness.

### 2. Learn from relative preference, without pretending missing means disliked

An implicit positive indicates observed engagement. An unobserved item may be unseen rather than disliked. BPR compares observed item i with training-unobserved item j for the same user:

<MathBlock latex="\Delta_{uij}=s(u,i)-s(u,j),\qquad \mathcal L=\frac1{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{(u,i,j)\in\mathcal T}\log(1+e^{-\Delta_{uij}})+\lambda\left(\operatorname{mean}(P^2)+\operatorname{mean}(Q^2)\right)" />

Here T is the set of training triples; P and Q are the trainable embedding tables; λ is the regularization coefficient. We state the mean-square convention because a sum penalty would require a different λ when table sizes change. Differentiate the softplus term:

<MathBlock latex="\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\Delta}=-\sigma(-\Delta),\quad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p_u}=-\sigma(-\Delta)(q_i-q_j),\quad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial q_i}=-\sigma(-\Delta)p_u,\quad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial q_j}=\sigma(-\Delta)p_u" />

At margin 0, loss is log 2 ≈ .6931 and derivative is −.5. At margin 3, loss is about .04859 and derivative is −.04743. Correctly ordered pairs still contribute smaller updates. `softplus(-margin)` avoids overflow from a naive exponential implementation.

The fixture enumerates all training-unobserved items rather than random negative sampling. No heldout labels enter this enumeration. Some future positives consequently act as training negatives: that is a real implicit-feedback limitation, not permission to consult the test set. Sampled softmax and in-batch contrastive learning are other objectives with different sampling corrections and false-negative issues; they are not implemented here.

### 3. Recall and NDCG need denominators that survive retrieval failures

Let Rᵤ be the complete heldout positive set, Cᵤ the ordered prediction list and k the cutoff. Recall measures how much of the known relevant set was retrieved. NDCG discounts low-ranked results and supports relevance grade r ≥ 0:

<MathBlock latex="\operatorname{Recall@k}(u)=\frac{|C_u[:k]\cap R_u|}{|R_u|},\qquad \operatorname{DCG@k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{2^{r_{C_{ut}}}-1}{\log_2(t+1)},\qquad \operatorname{NDCG@k}=\frac{\operatorname{DCG@k}}{\operatorname{IDCG@k}}" />

IDCG sorts the **full truth mapping**, not just labels found in the candidate list. Missing predictions contribute no gain; duplicate item predictions are rejected. Empty relevance or zero ideal gain returns zero in this API. Other benchmarks skip empty queries—report which convention you use. Aggregate with equal user weight, not equal interaction weight, and report the user count.

**Worked example:** prediction [9,2], positive set [2,3,4], k = 2. Recall = 1/3. DCG = 1/log₂3 ≈ .63093; IDCG = 1 + 1/log₂3 ≈ 1.63093; NDCG ≈ .38685. The missing positives cannot vanish from either denominator. A reranker's *conditional* NDCG on supplied candidates is a different diagnostic from end-to-end NDCG; label it separately.

<Gotcha>Exclude training-seen items before selecting top-k, not afterward. Filtering after truncation can return a needlessly short list and alter comparisons. Our contract sorts equal scores by ascending item ID, never returns masked items, and returns fewer than k items only when the eligible catalog is exhausted.</Gotcha>

### 4. Graph propagation is a train-time information boundary

Construct a bipartite adjacency A over user and item nodes from training positives only. D contains training node degrees; E⁽⁰⁾ stacks both embedding tables. This one-hop LightGCN-style variant uses symmetric degree normalization and averages propagated and original embeddings:

<MathBlock latex="\widetilde A=D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2},\qquad E^{(1)}=\widetilde A E^{(0)},\qquad E=\tfrac12(E^{(0)}+E^{(1)})" />

For edges (u₀,i₀), (u₀,i₁), (u₁,i₁), degrees are [2,1,1,2]. The coefficient from i₀ to u₀ is 1/√2 ≈ .7071; from i₁ to u₀ it is 1/2. A heldout (u₁,i₀) must remain absent in **both directions**. Isolated nodes have no incoming messages; clamp the zero degree for numerical safety but do not interpret random embeddings as a cold-start solution.

General message-passing GNNs can learn transformed neighbor messages, attention weights and node updates. This code deliberately implements only one normalized linear propagation step, inspired by LightGCN—not a full GCN, GAT or paper reproduction. The graph is dense for readability. Scaling requires sparse operations, sampling and careful oversmoothing/oversquashing analysis.

## The code, from scratch

First make the scalar meaning clear; then vectorize the same operation. Positive and negative tensors below contain item vectors for each training triple, not labels consulted from the holdout.

```python
# For one training triple:
margin = sum(user[j] * (positive[j] - negative[j]) for j in range(dim))
# For a batch, with each tensor shaped [triples, dim]:
margin_batch = (users * (positives - negatives)).sum(-1)
loss = torch.nn.functional.softplus(-margin_batch).mean()
```

The actual runnable project handles graph construction, L2, optimizer state and split validation:

```python
import torch
from two_tower_recsys import TwoTower, Popularity, temporal_split
from two_tower_recsys import synthetic_movielens, train, evaluate

torch.set_num_threads(1)
fit, held = temporal_split(synthetic_movielens())
print(evaluate(Popularity(fit, 18), fit, held, k=4))
for graph in (False, True):
    model = TwoTower(24, 18, graph_train=fit if graph else None)
    losses = train(model, fit, steps=100)
    print(losses[0], losses[-1], evaluate(model, fit, held, k=4))
```

The split yields 96 training and 24 heldout positive events. Both towers receive actual backpropagation updates. Tests pin metric denominators, empty relevance, score ties, seen exclusion, unknown-user fallback, split-time ties and train-only graph normalization. The notebook asserts that every heldout graph edge is zero. No accuracy number here is a real MovieLens benchmark.

`load_movielens(path)` optionally parses an **existing** modern `ratings.csv`, keeps ratings at least 4, and returns contiguous ID maps. It does not download data or support legacy `.dat` files. Repeated user/item positives are rejected by the splitter so you must choose an explicit aggregation/repeat-consumption policy. Per-user leave-last-out is not a global time split; real feature timestamps must also precede every prediction.

### Retrieval at scale, search and ads

An exact inner-product scan costs O(number of items × embedding dimension) per query. An approximate-nearest-neighbor index trades latency/memory for approximate retrieval; measure index recall against an exact search on a manageable sample. This lab is exact and tiny, not an ANN implementation. Item churn, deletions, index refresh and feature-schema changes create failure modes that an offline loss cannot detect.

Search adds explicit query intent, lexical matching and freshness: combine a lexical candidate source such as BM25 with semantic retrieval, then calibrate or learn fusion on heldout queries. Recommendation adds history, repetition and exploration. Ads add eligibility, bid/budget constraints, pacing, calibration and delayed conversions. A simple expected-value score might multiply a bid by a predicted click/conversion probability, but a raw BPR margin is **not** such a probability and this is not a complete auction design.

Clicks are affected by exposure and position, not only relevance. Do not train on unshown items as confirmed negative clicks, or infer causal product lift from offline NDCG. Log candidate versions, impressions, positions, timestamps and randomization/propensity information where lawful. Use user experience, provider diversity and safety guardrails rather than optimizing one engagement proxy without constraints.

### Graphs beyond recommendation

- **Fraud:** account/device/payment graphs expose relationships but require event-time edges, delayed labels and inductive evaluation; a future chargeback or shared investigation node can leak labels.
- **Molecules:** atoms/bonds have typed features and often require permutation-invariant graph readouts and geometric structure. Molecular scaffold splits test a different generalization question than random molecule splits.
- **Social systems:** link prediction and feed ranking encounter homophily, heterophily, privacy and interference. Neighbor exposure can violate independent-user experiment assumptions.
- **Recommendation:** collaborative graphs transfer interaction structure, but popularity amplification and sparse cold-start nodes remain. More message-passing layers are not automatically better.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Retrieval, ranking and final constraints remove different kinds of candidates; inspect the whole pipeline.
- Train both towers on training information only, with honest assumptions about implicit negatives.
- Preserve full relevance denominators, exclude seen items before top-k and define ties and empty cases.
- Graph edges and degrees are data; future relationships can leak even without labels.
- Cold-start slices, popularity baselines and causal experiments are essential companions to offline NDCG.
</KeyTakeaways>
